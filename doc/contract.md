# The contract

This is version 2.0.0 of the contract: what a conforming kernel promises for its booleans,
constructions and queries. Each clause has an id, which the runner's checks cite.

## Tolerance

Each check has a bound, written in terms of a linear tolerance ε in mm. ε is how far a kernel's
geometry may lie from the exact geometry. The suite's own tolerance is ε = 1e-7 mm, and a case
passes when its checks hold at that tolerance. Each check is also read at looser tolerances, as
[Tiers](#tiers) describes. A kernel built to a looser tolerance then shows where it stands instead
of only failing.

Every kernel is judged the same way, whatever tolerance it declares. An adapter sets every tolerance
setting its kernel offers to the suite's tolerance, 1e-7 mm, so that every kernel is asked for the
same thing. A setting that cannot be made that tight is set to the tightest value the kernel
supports.

In `hello` the adapter declares one tolerance: the loosest tolerance that still governs the kernel's
results. For example, a kernel that intersects at 1e-7 mm but builds its solids at 1e-5 declares
1e-5. In `configuration` the adapter lists every setting it changed from the kernel's default. Both
are reported beside the kernel's results as information only, and no check reads them.

Geometry that lies within ε of the exact geometry changes a volume by at most ε times the surface
area. A measured volume therefore meets its exact value within

    10 · ε · (area of both operands) + 1e-9 · (volume of both operands)

A measured area meets its exact value within a relative error of 1e-6. The factor of 10 allows for
the kernel's own measuring error, so an adapter measures with the kernel's most accurate
integration. A kernel's default integration can be looser than these bounds. For example, a fixed
rule can be off by 4e-4 of the volume on the spline faces of crossing cylinders, where an adaptive
rule on the same faces meets the bounds.

## Tiers

A tier is one of four tolerances: 1e-7, 1e-6, 1e-5 or 1e-3 mm. Results are counted at four levels:

| Level | What it is                                                 | Its result                                                |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Check | One comparison of the kernel's answer with what it must be | The tightest tolerance at which it holds                  |
| Case  | A few calls on one set of shapes, and the checks on them   | The loosest of its checks' tiers                          |
| Test  | A named group of cases, such as booleans of two boxes      | The count of its cases at each tier                       |
| Run   | Every case of the suite, on one kernel                     | The count of cases at each tier, and the share at 1e-7 mm |

A test's cases are generated by one or more families. A family is a generator of cases of one kind,
and [formats.md](formats.md#tests-and-case-ids) names them. A failed check fails only the case it
belongs to. A case is one piece of geometry, so it is wrong if any check on it fails. A test and a
run only count their cases.

**A check** that compares an error with a bound is judged at four tolerances: 1e-7, 1e-6, 1e-5 and
1e-3 mm. Its bound grows with the tolerance in the same way as the volume bound above. Its tier is
the tightest of the four tolerances at which it holds. Some checks do not depend on a tolerance,
such as a result's validity, its Betti numbers, a point placed on the wrong side, or a refusal that
was due. Such a check holds at every tier or at none.

**A case** takes the loosest of its checks' tiers. One check that holds only at 1e-3 mm makes the
case 1e-3, however many of its other checks hold at 1e-7. A case fails if any of its checks holds at
no tier. A case that ends without an answer has no tier: it is unsupported, refused, timed out or
crashed.

**A test** has no result of its own. For example, if a test has 60 cases, of which 59 hold at 1e-7
and one fails, it is reported as exactly that. One failed case does not fail the test.

**A run** counts its cases at each tier. Unlike a case, it never takes the loosest tier among them.
It has one total over every case, of all three kinds. The kinds are exact, where the definition
fixes one answer; sound, where the definition leaves the answer open within limits; and rejection,
where the input must be refused. The headline is the share of all cases in that total whose tier is
1e-7. For example, if a run has 20 cases, with 19 at 1e-7 and one at 1e-3, the headline is 95%, and
the one case appears in the count at 1e-3. A case at any other tier, a failed case and a case
without an answer all count against the headline.

A failed check is classed by what failed. If the check compared a measured value with a bound, it is
a failure of measurement: the volume, area, moment or distance the kernel reported is off. If the
check compared no measured value, it is a failure of geometry: the shape itself is wrong in its
points, its validity or its topology. A case with any failure of geometry is reported as a failure
of geometry.

## Outcomes

Each operation returns a valid result within the bounds, or refuses with a reason. A case's outcome
is the worst outcome among its operations and checks:

| Outcome     | Meaning                                                                           |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| pass        | Every operation completed and every check held at 1e-7 mm                         |
| unsupported | The kernel does not offer a function the case needs, or declined as unsupported   |
| refused     | An operation declined as invalid or failed; the checks that needed it are skipped |
| timeout     | An operation was still running at the time limit                                  |
| fail        | A check did not hold at 1e-7 mm: an invalid result, or a value outside its bound  |
| crash       | An operation panicked, or its worker died                                         |

A case that fails at 1e-7 mm may still hold at a looser tier, and a results file records both facts.
The case's `outcome` is the verdict at 1e-7 mm. Its `result` names the tier the case holds at (1e-6,
1e-5 or 1e-3), and is fail only when a check holds at none of them. [formats.md](formats.md#results)
describes both fields.

The outcomes rank in the order of the table above, worst last: a refusal declines openly, a timeout
never answers, a failed check misleads, and a crash takes the operation down.

Sound cases ([process.md](process.md#two-kinds-of-test)) check limits instead of one answer, and
have their own clauses. They count in the same total as every other case:

| Clause | Promise                                                                              |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Q1     | The kernel completes the act                                                         |
| Q2     | Its own checker finds the result valid                                               |
| Q3     | The result has the pieces, tunnels and voids it must                                 |
| Q4     | Outside the zone the definition leaves open, the result's volume is exact            |
| Q5     | The result's volume lies between the results at the act's largest and smallest sizes |
| Q6     | The blend meets its faces tangentially where it must touch them                      |
| Q7     | A loft passes through its sections: their outlines on its boundary, their areas cut  |
| Q8     | By its seams a curvature-continuous blend bends as what it meets does                |
| Q9     | The result holds none of the points the definition takes away                        |
| Q10    | Point for point, the result holds what every acceptable one holds and no more        |
| Q11    | Across the blend the boundary has no crease                                          |
| Q12    | Across a curvature-continuous blend the curvature carries on without a jump          |
| Q13    | The result is unchanged by the motions that leave the case unchanged                 |

Q10 to Q13 examine the result's shape itself, whereas Q5 bounds only its volume:

- **Q10, point for point.** The suite samples 8192 points of the shifted Halton sequence in the
  case's open zone, or in the base's box where the case has no open zone. It sets aside the points
  that the limits leave open and asks the kernel to classify the rest. The result must hold each
  point that every acceptable result holds, and must hold no point that no acceptable result holds.
  One point placed differently is allowed, as in B9.
- **Q11, no crease.** The suite casts a round grid of parallel rays, 24 across, at the blend. It
  asks for the normal at the point where each ray first meets the result. Where the normals of two
  neighbouring rays differ by more than 0.1 rad, it casts the ray between them, and repeats this on
  each half. The surface is smooth there if the normals of the halves come to agree. The surface has
  a crease if two points closer together than a thousandth of the blend's smallest radius still have
  normals that differ by more than 0.1 rad. Only a surface that bends at least a hundred times as
  tightly as the blend could differ that much over that distance.
- **Q12, no jump in curvature.** The suite runs the same scan on the principal curvatures. A jump is
  a change of more than a quarter of one over the radius, within a distance of a hundredth of the
  radius.
- **Q13, symmetry.** The suite moves every ray of the grid by each motion the case names and casts
  it again. It must stop where it did, within a thousandth of the radius. A case names the motions
  that leave its solid and its named edges unchanged within the grid. These are the swaps of a
  square corner's three edges, where the three are blended alike, and the mirror in the plane
  between two faces, two arms or two sides that are alike there.

Three more rules apply to these checks:

- A point where the kernel gives no normal, such as a point on an edge, does not count as a crease
  by itself. It is halved round like any other.
- A kernel that gives no normal at more than a fiftieth of the grid's points fails Q11.
- Q11 to Q13 are skipped for a kernel that offers no `raycast` or no `normal`. A kernel with no
  `curvature` fails Q12, as it fails Q8.

A primitive operand meets B0 when its volume and area meet their closed forms. B0 is therefore also
the contract for the constructions that make primitives from a profile:

- An extrusion's volume is the profile's area times the height.
- A revolution's volume, and a screw's, is the profile's first moment about the axis times the angle
  (Pappus). A screw's rise moves the profile within its own plane, so it sweeps no volume.
- A ruled loft between a profile and its copy scaled by s has the volume h·A·(1 + s + s²)/3.

The areas of screwed and lofted sides are integrals, which the suite evaluates by Gauss quadrature
to well inside the bound.

A built operand is a boolean of other operands. It meets B0 when its volume meets the suite's
sampled estimate within B8's bound. If the kernel fails to build one, the case is refused.

B8 and B9 do not need any kernel to know the answer. The suite itself tests whether a point is in
each exact primitive. It samples the box that both operands' boxes share, at 4096 points of a Halton
sequence shifted by irrational amounts. B8 allows five standard errors of a random sample of that
size, two points' share of the box, and the volume bound. B9 allows one point placed differently. B8
catches a wrong volume, whether the geometry is wrong or the measurement of it is. B9 catches wrong
geometry alone.

Every clause that asks the kernel where sample points lie treats the kernel's classifier the same
way. If a kernel offers no `classify`, or refuses the call as `unsupported`, the suite does not ask,
and the check is left out. If a kernel offers `classify` but gives no answer for a solid, it fails
the check. Giving no answer means refusing as `failed` or `invalid`, running out of time, or
crashing. The reason is that a result whose points the kernel cannot place has not been shown to be
the right solid.

The suite's membership test follows each definition in [api.md](api.md). A profile's region is
tested by winding number: exactly for lines and arcs, and from a fine polygon for a spline. For the
swept solids:

- A point is in a revolution when its angle lies in `[0, angle]` and its `(ρ, w)` lies in the
  region.
- A point is in a screw when `(ρ, w − rise·φ)` lies in the region at some turn `φ ≤ angle` congruent
  to the point's angle.
- A point is in a loft when `(u/σ, v/σ)` lies in the region, at its height.

Membership in a boolean follows from membership in its operands.

### Moments and distance

B10 to B12 judge `centroid` and `inertia` through the raw moments they give: `∭ r dV` and
`∭ r rᵀ dV` about the world origin. Raw moments add over pieces as volume does. Every primitive's
raw moments are known exactly. Each kind of primitive is an extrusion, revolution, screw or loft of
a profile, and its moments reduce to the profile's area moments by Green's theorem and one integral
along the sweep. Geometry within ε changes a first moment by at most the volume bound times `R`, and
a second moment by at most the volume bound times `R²`. Here `R` is the greatest distance from the
origin to any point of either operand. Those two limits are the bounds. B12's sampled estimate
allows five standard errors per component, as B8 does.

B13 allows `10·ε + 1e-9·R`. A family states the exact distance where it knows it: zero for operands
that overlap or touch, and the gap for the `separated` family. Wherever the sample finds a point in
both operands, the distance must be zero. Distance to an empty solid is undefined, so the suite does
not ask for it.

## Results that touch

A boolean's result is the regularized set operation: the closure of the interior of the set result.
Its separate pieces may touch at points or along curves, and such a result is valid (B1). For
example, when a cylinder is cut by an equal cylinder crossing it at a right angle, its two halves
touch at two points. Pieces that share an area of face are one solid, because that area is interior
to the regularized result, and they must be returned merged. A piece may also touch itself, as a
block with a bore flush to one face does along that line. A piece that passes into another piece, or
into itself, is invalid. The kernel may represent a contact as it likes: as one edge or vertex that
several faces share, or as copies of it. The Betti numbers are those of the point set, so pieces
that only touch count as one piece. A kernel that refuses a touching result scores refused, not
pass.

STEP's solids are manifold, so a touching result is written as the manifold boundary of its
interior. Each piece is a solid of its own, and each side of a contact has its own vertices and
edges. Exchange files are commonly written this way. The solids in a file are together one part's
shape, so an adapter reads them back as their union, which is the result again (B17).

### Blends and exchange

A fillet or chamfer case blends edges of a prism, or the rims of a cylinder or cone. The case names
the solid the blend must give. That solid is either the prism on the profile with those corners
rounded or cut, or the revolution of the meridian with those corners rounded or cut. B14 to B16
judge the result against that solid's closed forms and membership, as B0, B9 and B6 do for
primitives. B17 sends two kinds of result through the kernel's own STEP writer and reader: a ∪ b of
every pair case, and every blend's result.

### How the later clauses are tested

This section says how each of the following clauses is tested. A clause not listed here is checked
as its row in the table above says:

- **B18, refusals.** The kernel is given an input that [api.md](api.md#refusals) rules out, and it
  passes only when it refuses the input. The fault is in the shape itself (such as a profile that
  crosses itself), in one number (such as a radius of zero), or in a point that names no edge or
  face.
- **B19, Betti numbers.** The suite asks for the Betti numbers where the case knows them. It knows
  them from a primitive's kind, a drilled plate's holes, a solid inside a box, two solids apart, the
  union of two solids that touch (one piece), and a bore flush to a face (a tunnel).
- **B20, repeatability.** `a ∩ b` is asked for twice in one worker, and the volumes and areas are
  compared bit for bit.
- **B21, section area.** `a ∩ b` is cut by the plane through the middle of its sample box that is
  perpendicular to a direction drawn from the case. The area must meet a sampled estimate over that
  plane, within five standard errors and two points' share.
- **B23, meshes.** `a ∩ b` is meshed within 0.01 mm. Its volume may differ by at most twice that
  tolerance times its area. That is twice what `mesh` promises, which allows for the kernel's own
  measuring error.
- **B24, a solid with itself** (`self-booleans`). A primitive is combined with itself: the same
  handle is passed as both operands. This clause has a family of its own, so that a kernel that
  refuses such a pair does not lose every other boolean.
- **B25, similarities** (`transforms`). Half the cases are rigid motions, a quarter are scaled and a
  quarter are mirrored. This clause has a family of its own, so that a kernel without one does not
  lose every other case.
- **B26, rays.** A ray is cast through `a ∪ b` from outside its box. The suite sorts the meetings
  the kernel lists and treats meetings within `10·ε` of each other as one crossing. It compares the
  crossings with its own march along the ray, which it refines by bisection to 1e-12 of the ray's
  length. Families whose features may be thinner than a step of the march, or may touch, are left
  out.
- **B27, assemblies** (`assemblies`). The suite makes each part on its frame moved to where the part
  stands. It asks [`interference`](api.md#interference) about one pair at a time, and leaves out the
  pairs whose bounding balls are farther apart than the clearance. A pair clashes when the kernel's
  answer has a volume above 0 or a gap below the clearance.
- **B32, stretches and shears** (`stretches`). The cases are [`affine`](api.md#affine) maps. A third
  are stretched along axes by unequal factors, a third are also sheared, and a third are also
  mirrored. The volume, moments and placed points have closed forms. The area has none, so it must
  lie between the singular-value bounds.
- **B33, bounding boxes** (`bounding-boxes`). The cases ask for the box round turned boxes,
  cylinders, frustums, balls and rings, alone or joined. A primitive's least box has a closed form,
  and a union's least box is the box round both.
- **B34, splits** (`splits`). Any primitive is cut through the middle of its box at a slant. The
  pieces' volumes must add up to the solid's, and every sample point must lie in the piece for its
  side.
- **B35, sections** (`sections`). A ball, a cylinder's side, a box, or a ring is cut level or
  through its axis. Each section is circles, an ellipse or a convex polygon. The suite joins the
  pieces the kernel returns end to end, treating ends within `10·ε` of each other as one point. An
  end that meets no other end fails the case. Every point must lie within ten times the tolerance of
  both the plane and the boundary. Chords may fall short of the curves' length by at most `s/(3ρ)`
  of it, where `s` is the chord tolerance and `ρ` is the least radius of curvature.
- **B36, sewing** (`sewing`). A prism's faces are written as loose open shells. Each is shifted
  along its normal by up to 1e-4 mm, and a quarter of them are turned inside out. They are then sewn
  within 2e-3 mm. The volume may change by at most the shift times the area.

- **B40, nearest points** (`nearest-points`, `nearest-in-holes`). The cases call
  [`closest`](api.md#closest) on a turned box, cylinder, frustum, ball or ring. The query points lie
  all about the solid, inside and outside it, and on its axis beyond each end, where a ball has its
  poles and a cone has its apex. Each answer must lie on the solid and be as far from its query
  point as the closed form says. Where several points are equally near, any of them passes. For a
  block drilled through, queried from points in the hole, the nearest point is the one point of the
  hole's wall straight out from the axis, and the answer must be that point.
- **B41, points on the boundary** (`boundary-points`). The cases call [`classify`](api.md#classify)
  on a turned primitive, at points of its faces, edges and rims, and at points a hundredth of a
  millimetre to either side of a face. The points of the first kind are `"on"`. The points to either
  side are `"in"` and `"out"`.
- **B42, displays** (`displays`). The cases call [`display`](api.md#display) on a box, a cylinder, a
  ball, a cone, a drilled box, a box whose four upright edges are rounded, or a box drawn as a
  prism with one side in two faces, within 0.01 mm. The faces, once joined, must close, with every
  edge used once each way round, and must bound the volume within twice the tolerance times the
  area. Every vertex must lie within 1e-5 of the solid's size of one of its surfaces, with that
  surface's outward normal there to within 1e-3, and every triangle must face the way its normals
  do. A vertex at the cone's apex must, in each triangle that uses it, have to within 1e-3 one of
  the normals the cone has across that triangle halfway out from the apex; a triangle of no area is
  passed over. The edges are judged in space by the rules B38 applies to a drawing, against the
  curves where two of the solid's surfaces meet, at an angle or smoothly: every vertex lies on one,
  every chord lies within the tolerance of one, every one is covered, and the edges have no more
  length than those curves have. A ball has no such curve, a cone's one is its rim, and the rounded
  box has eight where a round meets a side smoothly. An edge flagged in `edge_split`, and a seam,
  are no part of this.

  What each edge says of its faces is a check of its own. Each edge must name at least two faces
  in `edge_faces`, every one a face of the display, and each of its vertices must lie within twice
  the tolerance of each face it names. An edge between two distinct faces must give in `edge_turns`
  the turn the surfaces make there, to within 1e-3 at both ends of its range: `π/2` along a box's
  edges, a cylinder's rims and a hole's, `π/2` and the cone's half angle at a cone's rim, and 0
  where a round meets a side. An edge flagged in `edge_split` must lie on the solid's surfaces, off
  every curve where two of them meet, between two distinct faces, with a turn of 0 if it gives
  one. The prism's side may come back as one face or as two: an edge drawn between the two must be
  flagged. Every seam must lie on the solid's surfaces. A cylinder, a cone and a drilled box each
  have one seam, or none where the kernel cuts the round face in two by split edges instead; a
  box, a rounded box and the prism have none; a ball's seams are not counted. A display that names
  no face, gives no turn, flags no split and lists no seam, as an adapter written to protocol 1.0.0
  does, fails this check alone, as not reported. A ball is the exception: it has no edge to report,
  so its display passes this check with nothing said.

The face edits `delete_faces`, `move_faces` and `thicken` are treated as features, so B14 to B16
judge each result as the primitive it must be. `sweep_path` is treated as a constructor, so B0 to
B13 judge it. Its closed forms are integrals along the path.

### STEP

B22 gives the kernel a STEP file that the suite itself writes: a prism of planar faces in AP214. It
judges what the kernel reads against the prism. The solid read must be valid, have the prism's
volume and area, and place the sample points as the prism does. The file's meaning comes from the
format, not from another kernel. B17, by contrast, sends a kernel's own output back through its own
reader. A kernel can pass B17 while writing files that no other reader understands.

When the suite is run with `--peer`, a second kernel reads what the first writes. The suite reports
whether the second kernel reads the solid that the suite's sample describes. This is reported as
interop, apart from the score. A mismatch may be the fault of either kernel, so it counts against
neither.

### What an adapter may do

**An adapter answers each function with the kernel's one call for it, or the function is
unsupported.** An adapter never composes one function from others, and it never makes a decision. A
threshold, a vote between answers, a retry with other settings and a repair of the result are all
decisions. For example, a split made from booleans with a half-space would score the booleans twice
and credit the kernel with a split it does not have.

**`unsupported` means one thing: the kernel has not built this.** Either the kernel lacks the
function, or it lacks the function for arguments of this sort. An example of the second is a mirror
in a kernel that only moves solids rigidly. An input that has no answer is `invalid`. A call that
should have worked and did not is `failed`.

A kernel may well be able to reach the same answer another way. It could get a section's area from a
boolean with a half-space, a ray's crossings by firing again from each crossing, or a distance by
classifying a point after the solve. Kernels differ in how they are built, as browsers do, and that
is not a fault. But a suite can only compare kernels on functions they offer in the same form. An
adapter that builds a missing function out of other functions becomes a second, untested kernel
standing in for the first, and its results say nothing about either. Such a function is therefore
`unsupported`. Each adapter says why in one line, and the kernel is judged on what it offers
directly.

An adapter may do five things that are not a call of the kernel. No others are allowed:

1. **Translating form.** The adapter may give a profile's segments as the kernel's curves, a frame
   as its placement, and a path as its spine. It may sample a curve the kernel returns into points,
   sort a split's solids by side, and move a solid that the kernel builds at the origin onto its
   frame.
2. **Addressing a point.** When a point names an edge or a face, the adapter turns it into the
   kernel's own address for that edge or face: the nearest entity and its parameters there. It does
   this through the kernel's distance query at the contract's `10·ε`, and uses no number of its own.
   Where a kernel answers on one face and the point lies on several, as a point on an edge does, the
   adapter does not choose between them: the call is `unsupported`.
3. **The empty solid.** A kernel that has no empty solid says in its own way that a result is empty.
   The adapter reads that statement, and no other refusal, as the empty solid. The adapter itself
   answers queries on an empty solid: a volume of 0, no meetings of a ray, and every point outside.
4. **What a handle means.** A handle is a solid that stays as it was. Where a kernel's call consumes
   its operand, the adapter keeps a record of how the solid was made and makes it again.
5. **Drawing what the kernel made.** `mesh`, `display`, `section` and `project` draw a solid the
   kernel has already made, and make nothing. For these the adapter may make several of the
   kernel's calls that read the solid without changing it, such as its mesher, its list of the
   faces that use an edge and its normal at a point, and put their answers into the protocol's
   form. It may work out what follows from those answers alone: the turn at an edge from the two
   normals the kernel gave there, a seam from a face the kernel says uses an edge twice, a split
   edge from two faces the kernel says lie on one surface, and a curve's points by the kernel's
   own sampler where it has one. It moves no point, makes no normal and compares no surfaces with
   a number of its own. What the kernel cannot say, such as an edge's turn, the adapter leaves
   out.

Where a case asks for one thing by way of another, the suite does the other thing itself. It uses
one recipe that is public and the same for every kernel. The recipes are the stand-ins below, the
pairing of an assembly's parts, the merging of a ray's meetings at an edge, the joining of a
section's pieces, and the welding of a mesh's vertices. A test of a function itself never takes a
recipe: a kernel without the function is `unsupported` there, and only there.

In a kernel's `hello`, `configuration` states every setting the adapter gives the kernel that is not
the kernel's default, such as a tolerance, a mode of integration or a mesh's angular deflection. An
adapter that sets nothing states nothing.

A kernel's `hello` leaves out the functions the kernel does not offer, and the suite does not ask
for them. A case that needs such a function is `unsupported`. In the other cases, the checks that
the function would feed are left out.

### Stand-ins for the five primitives

A case about a boolean or a fillet starts from a box or a sphere, and its result should not depend
on whether the kernel has a constructor for that primitive. So where a kernel lists `box`,
`cylinder`, `sphere`, `torus` or `cone` as unsupported, the suite sends the same solid as an
extrusion or a revolution in its place:

| Primitive  | Sent instead                                                                        |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `box`      | `extrude` of the rectangle from the origin to `size[0]`, `size[1]`, by `size[2]`    |
| `cylinder` | `extrude` of the circle of its radius about the origin, by its height               |
| `sphere`   | `revolve`, a whole turn, of the half disc right of the axis                         |
| `torus`    | `revolve`, a whole turn, of the tube's circle, its centre `major` out from the axis |
| `cone`     | `revolve`, a whole turn, of the meridian: the axis, the two radii and the side      |

The suite makes the stand-in, and it is the same one for every kernel. The adapter still only
answers the call it is sent. If a kernel also lacks the stand-in's function, it cannot make the
solid, and the case is `unsupported`.

The constructors' own tests are the exception. These are `constructions/box`, `cylinder`, `sphere`,
`torus` and `cone`, and a test that a constructor refuses a bad solid. They ask for the constructor
and nothing else, so a kernel without the constructor is `unsupported` there. The results name each
stand-in a run used, in `stand_ins`.

### Stand-ins for a mirror image and a placed part

Two more kinds of case ask for one thing by way of another. The suite makes that other thing itself
where the kernel cannot:

- **A part joined to its mirror image** (`mirror-joins`, `chain-across-a-mirror-join`). The suite
  asks for the image with `transform`. Where the kernel refuses the mirror as `unsupported`, the
  suite makes the image as a primitive of the same kind. The primitive's frame is mirrored, with x
  turned back to keep it right-handed, and its box or profile is mirrored across that x to match.
  The union, or the fillet across the join, is then judged as it is for any kernel. `transforms`
  remains the test of a mirror itself.
- **The parts of an assembly** (`assemblies`). Each part is made on its frame moved to where the
  part stands, and is never moved after it is made. The interference test therefore does not depend
  on `transform`.

### Time

Each call has a time limit, which is 120 s unless the run sets another. A call that passes the limit
times out, and its worker is replaced. A case's time is the sum of its calls' wall times, as the
client measures them. A run is one pass. A test's time is the median of its cases' times, leaving
out the cases that gave no answer. A test with fewer than five timed cases is marked as low
confidence. Two kernels' times are comparable only on the cases both pass at the same tier, and only
when both ran on the same machine. The run also times a calibration workload on its kernel and
machine: two crossing cylinders, their intersection and its volume. That time is recorded for
comparing machines, never kernels.

## Known limits

- **Measuring.** B0 to B6 trust each kernel's own volume and area, and a kernel's integration can be
  off where its geometry is right. A fixed rule can measure 4e-4 of the volume off on crossing
  cylinders, and an adaptive rule can be 5% off on a periodic spline prism while reporting an error
  estimate of 1e-17. B8 and B9 now separate such errors from wrong geometry, but only to their
  sampling bound.
- **Time budgets.** A call has a time limit, but no budget that scales with its work. A boolean of
  two boxes and a boolean of two splines may each take the same 120 s. Work counters reported by the
  kernel would let budgets scale with the case.
- **Tolerance scale.** ε is absolute. The `scaled` family's parts range from microns to tens of
  metres, so they test whether a kernel holds one tolerance at every size or scales its own. Whether
  the contract should declare ε relative to a part's size is an open question.
